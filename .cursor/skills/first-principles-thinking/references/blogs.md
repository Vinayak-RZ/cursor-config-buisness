## 1. Farnam Street — What is First Principles Thinking?

Source: [fs.blog/first-principles](https://fs.blog/first-principles/)

**Claim.** First principles thinking separates what is absolutely true from assumption, then rebuilds. You do not need atomic physics; going one or two layers deeper than most people already opens new designs.

**Lego / bicycle image.** A Lego house can be nudged (analogy / continuous improvement) or broken into bricks and rebuilt as something else (first principles). A bicycle is seat, chain, frame, bars — or, deeper, metals that could become a different object.

**Feynman warning.** Knowledge learned by rote is fragile. Understanding means knowing what to do.

**Techniques FS recommends**

1. **Socratic questioning** (systematic, not vibes):
   - Clarify: Why do I think this? What exactly do I think?
   - Challenge assumptions: How do I know this is true? What if the opposite?
   - Evidence: How can I back this up? What are the sources?
   - Alternatives: What might others think?
   - Consequences: What if I am wrong?
   - Question the question: Was I asking the right thing?
2. **Five Whys** — keep asking until you hit a fundamental or an unknown you can measure. Children's "why?" is first principles in the wild.

**Business-relevant FS point.** Everything that is not a law of nature is a shared belief (money, borders, "how our industry works"). Treat those as challengeable. When the environment changes and you keep acting as if it hasn't, you pay for it.

**Use in answers.** Name the layer you stopped at. If you only rearranged the Lego house, say so and go one layer deeper.

## 2. James Clear — First Principles (Elon Musk / think for yourself)

Source: [jamesclear.com/first-principles](https://jamesclear.com/first-principles)

**Definition.** A first principle is a basic assumption that cannot be deduced further. Aristotle: "the first basis from which a thing is known." In practice: think like a scientist — what are we sure is true?

**SpaceX example (use sparingly — see anti-patterns).** Quoted rocket price ~$65M. First-principles decomposition: aerospace aluminum, titanium, copper, carbon fiber. Commodity value of materials ~2% of typical price. Implication: the gap is process, regulation, and incumbents — not physics. SpaceX built rather than bought.

**John Boyd snowmobile.** Deconstruct a motorboat, a tank, and a bicycle into parts; recombine into a snowmobile. Innovation is often recombination of known parts, not magic.

**Gutenberg.** Wine press + movable type + paper + ink. The best solution is often not where everyone is already looking.

**Form vs function (suitcase).** Wheels existed for millennia; rolling luggage appeared ~1970. People optimized bag *form* (better leather, zippers, nylon) instead of the *function* (move belongings). "Where are the flying cars?" misses that airplanes already do the function of flight.

**Clear's fork**

| If you want… | Use |
|--------------|-----|
| Enhance an existing process | Continuous improvement inside the current form |
| Think for yourself / new category | First principles: put function in front, abandon inherited form |

**Use in answers.** Ask: are we improving the bicycle or building the snowmobile? Are we optimizing form ("an app that looks like Slack") or function (the job)?

## 3. StartupShortcut — First Principles Thinking for Business

Source: [startupshortcut.com/knowledge-base/first-principles-thinking](https://startupshortcut.com/knowledge-base/first-principles-thinking)

**Battery example.** Packs ~$600/kWh by convention. Materials on the commodity market ~$80/kWh. Gap = manufacturing and scale, not a physical floor. That reasoning justified Tesla's Gigafactory bet.

**Analogy vs first principles (for business)**

| | Analogy | First principles |
|--|---------|------------------|
| Start | What others charge / build | What is true (value, cost, behavior) |
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Risk | Copy others' errors | Novel errors |
| Best for | Routine, proven domains | Novel problems, strategy, disruption |

**Socratic pattern for operators**

1. Why do we do it this way?
2. Is that reason still valid?
3. What assumptions are embedded?
4. What would we do if starting from scratch?
5. What is physically / economically / logically necessary vs merely conventional?

**Worked business applications**

- **Pricing:** Do not start at competitor $49/mo. Start from value created, cost to serve, willingness to pay vs alternatives (hire a person, duct-tape tools, do nothing).
- **Product:** Job to be done + simplest way to complete it. Competitor feature lists are analogy.
- **GTM:** Shortest path from problem-awareness to first value. "We need a sales team because SaaS companies have sales teams" is analogy.
- **Hiring:** Outcomes required vs cargo-cult org charts from bigger companies.

**When analogy wins:** legal compliance, standard accounting, speed > optimality, long-stable patterns, analysis cost > benefit of novelty.

**Practice drills:** Five Whys on a process; blank-page "company does not exist yet"; cost decomposition of the largest cost line; customer job analysis instead of feature requests.

## 4. Farnam Street — Elon Musk: A Framework for Thinking

Source: [fs.blog/elon-musk-framework-thinking](https://fs.blog/elon-musk-framework-thinking/)

Two complementary tools (do not turn every memo into a SpaceX essay):

1. **Physics-style first principles** — boil to fundamentals; reason up. Analogy is how you get through the day; physics is how you do something *new*.
2. **Solicit negative feedback**, especially from friends. Almost nobody does this. Pair with `strategy-red-team` / `pre-mortem` when the rebuild is high-stakes.

Musk's **algorithm** (useful for ops and product bloat, from his later public comments — apply as a checklist, not mythology):

1. Question every requirement (who required it? why?)
2. Delete / simplify (you will not delete enough)
3. Optimize (only after 1–2)
4. Accelerate
5. Automate last

Wrong order (automate a dumb process) is a common agent failure.
